# Socioeconomic and Geographic Inequalities in Anaemia Among Women of Reproductive Age in Ghana

## Abstract

### Background
Anaemia remains common among Ghanaian women of reproductive age, but national estimates may conceal socioeconomic and geographic inequalities. We quantified wealth-related inequality in anaemia and examined its measured contributors and community-level variation.

### Methods
We analysed nationally representative data for women aged 15–49 years with valid anaemia measurements. Survey-weighted prevalence, Erreygers-corrected concentration indices, decomposition analysis, survey-weighted logistic regression, and multilevel logistic models were used.

### Results
Among 7,557 women, anaemia prevalence was **41.1% (95% CI 39.6–42.6%)**, declining from **46.6%** in the poorest wealth quintile to **38.8%** in the richest. The Erreygers index was **-0.0589** (95% bootstrap CI **-0.0914 to -0.0212**), indicating concentration among poorer women. BMI/nutritional status contributed **77.8%** of measured inequality. Pregnancy was associated with higher adjusted odds of anaemia (aOR **1.77**, 95% CI **1.43–2.21**). Regional and residual community-level heterogeneity persisted after adjustment.

### Conclusions
Anaemia in Ghana is socioeconomically and geographically patterned. Equity-focused, nutritionally informed, and geographically targeted interventions are warranted.

**Keywords:** anaemia; women of reproductive age; socioeconomic inequality; concentration index; Ghana

## Introduction

Anaemia remains a major public-health concern among women of reproductive age, particularly in low- and lower-middle-income settings. Globally, women and pregnant women remain among the groups most affected. Anaemia is multifactorial, arising from nutritional deficiencies, infection and inflammation, reproductive factors, and inherited conditions, and its distribution is therefore shaped by both biological and social determinants.

Ghana continues to experience a high burden. National estimates indicate that about two in five women of reproductive age were anaemic in 2022. Conventional analyses identify individual correlates of anaemia, but national averages and determinant models alone do not show how the burden is distributed across the socioeconomic hierarchy or how much unexplained variation persists across communities.

Health-inequality methods offer a complementary perspective. Concentration curves and concentration indices quantify socioeconomic inequality across the full wealth distribution, while decomposition methods estimate the statistical contribution of measured characteristics to that inequality. Multilevel models can additionally quantify contextual heterogeneity between communities.

This study examined socioeconomic and geographic inequalities in anaemia among women aged 15–49 years in Ghana. We estimated national and subgroup prevalence, quantified wealth-related inequality, decomposed that inequality into measured contributors, estimated adjusted associations, and assessed residual community-level heterogeneity.

## Methods

### Study design and population
We conducted a secondary cross-sectional analysis of the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey. The survey used a stratified multistage sampling design. The Individual Recode file contained 15,014 women aged 15–49 years; 7,557 with a valid anaemia classification comprised the main analytic sample.

### Outcome and explanatory variables
The primary outcome was any anaemia, defined from the DHS anaemia classification as mild, moderate, or severe anaemia versus no anaemia.

Socioeconomic position was measured using the household wealth index. Wealth quintiles were used in descriptive and regression analyses, while the continuous wealth score was used to rank women for inequality analysis.

Prespecified covariates were age, education, residence, region, pregnancy status, parity, BMI category, employment, and marital status.

### Survey analysis
Population-level estimates incorporated the DHS women's sampling weight, primary sampling unit, and sampling stratum. Weighted prevalence estimates and 95% confidence intervals were calculated overall and across participant characteristics.

### Inequality analysis
Women were ranked from poorest to richest using the continuous wealth score. Because anaemia is a bounded binary outcome, the Erreygers-corrected concentration index was used as the primary measure of wealth-related inequality. Negative values indicate concentration of anaemia among poorer women. Stratified PSU bootstrap resampling was used to estimate uncertainty.

The corrected index was decomposed using a survey-weighted linear probability model to estimate contributions from age, education, residence, pregnancy, parity, BMI, employment, and marital status. Wealth itself was not entered as a determinant because it defined the socioeconomic ranking.

### Regression and multilevel analysis
Survey-weighted logistic regression estimated adjusted associations with anaemia. The full model included age group, education, wealth quintile, residence, pregnancy, parity, BMI category, employment, marital status, and region.

Random-intercept logistic models with women nested within DHS clusters quantified community-level heterogeneity. ICCs and median odds ratios were calculated, and proportional change in cluster variance compared null and adjusted models.

## Results

Anaemia prevalence was **41.1% (95% CI 39.6–42.6%)**. Prevalence was **46.6%** in the poorest wealth quintile and **38.8%** in the richest. It was also higher among rural women than urban women (**43.4% vs 39.4%**), among currently pregnant women (**51.4% vs 40.4%**), and among underweight women (**49.3%**) compared with women with obesity (**33.9%**).

The Erreygers-corrected concentration index was **-0.0589** (95% bootstrap CI **-0.0914 to -0.0212**), confirming disproportionate concentration of anaemia among poorer women (Figure 1).

BMI/nutritional status contributed the largest measured share of inequality (**77.8%**), followed by education (**17.3%**). The BMI contribution remained negative in bootstrap analysis and was similar when BMI was represented categorically.

In the fully adjusted model, currently pregnant women had higher odds of anaemia than women who were not pregnant or unsure (aOR **1.77**, 95% CI **1.43–2.21**). Compared with normal-weight women, overweight women had lower adjusted odds (aOR **0.73**, 95% CI **0.62–0.87**) and women with obesity had lower adjusted odds (aOR **0.60**, 95% CI **0.49–0.72**).

Regional heterogeneity persisted. Relative to Greater Accra, Oti had higher adjusted odds (aOR **1.48**, 95% CI **1.11–1.98**) and Bono lower adjusted odds (aOR **0.61**, 95% CI **0.41–0.90**). Regional prevalence ranged from approximately **30.1% in Bono** to **51.8% in Oti** (Figure 2).

The null multilevel model had an ICC of **4.35%** and MOR of **1.45**. After adjustment, the ICC was **3.14%** and MOR **1.37**. Measured characteristics explained approximately **28.8%** of between-cluster variance.

## Discussion

### Main finding of this study

Anaemia affected about two in five Ghanaian women of reproductive age and was disproportionately concentrated among poorer women. Nutritional status accounted for the largest measured component of socioeconomic inequality, while pregnancy and region remained prominent correlates after adjustment. Meaningful regional and community-level heterogeneity persisted beyond measured individual characteristics.

### What is already known on this topic

Anaemia remains common among women in Ghana and elsewhere in sub-Saharan Africa. Previous Ghanaian work has documented associations with pregnancy, parity, nutritional status, wealth, and geography. These studies have established the scale of the problem but have generally emphasized prevalence and individual-level determinants.

### What this study adds

This analysis shows that the burden is not merely high but unequally distributed across the socioeconomic hierarchy. The concentration-index approach captures inequality across the entire wealth distribution, while decomposition indicates that the social patterning of nutritional status is strongly linked to the observed inequality.

The attenuation of wealth coefficients in the adjusted regression does not contradict the concentration-index result. Socioeconomic position is upstream of several characteristics entered into the model; population-level inequality can therefore remain evident even when the conditional wealth coefficient becomes weak after adjustment.

The study also demonstrates that geographic heterogeneity is not fully explained by individual characteristics. Residual community-level variation suggests that contextual factors such as local food environments, infection patterns, health-service access, sanitation, or environmental exposures may contribute.

### Limitations of this study

The cross-sectional design prevents causal inference. Haemoglobin identifies anaemia but does not establish its cause, so iron deficiency cannot be distinguished from infection, inflammation, haemoglobinopathies, blood loss, or other micronutrient deficiencies. BMI is an imperfect proxy for nutritional status and the inverse association with anaemia should not be interpreted as evidence that excess body weight is protective. Decomposition estimates are model-dependent and represent statistical contributions rather than causal pathways.

The primary outcome also uses the anaemia classification embedded in the 2022 DHS. WHO subsequently updated haemoglobin cut-offs and measurement guidance, so future work should assess the effect of newer definitions on prevalence and inequality estimates.

## Conclusion

Anaemia among women of reproductive age in Ghana is both socioeconomically and geographically patterned. Poorer women bear a disproportionate burden, with nutritional status accounting for a large share of the measured inequality. Persistent regional and community-level heterogeneity supports equity-focused, nutritionally informed, and geographically targeted prevention strategies.
