# Discussion Draft

## Principal findings

In this nationally representative analysis of Ghanaian women aged 15–49 years, approximately two in five women were anaemic. The burden was not evenly distributed. Anaemia prevalence was higher among women in poorer households, those with less education, rural residents, currently pregnant women, and underweight women, and it varied considerably across regions. The Erreygers-corrected concentration index was negative, confirming that anaemia was disproportionately concentrated among socioeconomically disadvantaged women.

The decomposition analysis adds an important layer to these descriptive gradients. BMI and nutritional status accounted for the largest measured share of wealth-related inequality, with education, residence, parity, and pregnancy status contributing smaller shares. In the fully reproducible specification, the BMI contribution remained large and its 200-replicate bootstrap interval stayed entirely below zero, whereas intervals for the smaller domains included zero. In the adjusted survey-weighted regression, pregnancy, BMI category, and region remained the strongest correlates, whereas the conditional associations for wealth and education attenuated substantially. This distinction is important: population-level socioeconomic inequality can remain pronounced even when a direct wealth coefficient is weak after adjustment, because wealth-related disparities may operate through the unequal distribution of nutritional, reproductive, and geographic characteristics.

## Comparison with previous evidence

The overall anaemia prevalence of 41.1% is consistent with the 2022 national estimate and with a recent analysis of the same survey that reported prevalence of 40.4% among non-pregnant women and 51.4% among pregnant women. That study identified parity, BMI, wealth, and geographic zone among important correlates. Our findings agree with the strong roles of pregnancy, nutritional status, and geography, but extend previous work by quantifying socioeconomic inequality directly, decomposing the inequality into measured contributors, and estimating residual cluster-level heterogeneity.

The higher odds of anaemia among pregnant women are biologically plausible because pregnancy increases iron requirements and expands plasma volume, while pre-existing nutritional deficiencies may become more apparent during gestation. WHO continues to identify pregnant women as one of the groups most affected by anaemia globally. The result should nevertheless be interpreted as an association rather than as evidence that pregnancy alone causes anaemia, because the cross-sectional survey cannot fully capture pre-pregnancy nutritional status, infection, supplementation adherence, or other clinical factors.

The inverse pattern between BMI and anaemia was one of the most prominent findings. Underweight women had the highest descriptive prevalence, while overweight and obesity were associated with lower adjusted odds relative to normal weight. This pattern has also been reported in other analyses of anaemia among women, including recent Ghanaian evidence. However, the finding must not be interpreted as suggesting that excess body weight is beneficial. BMI is an imperfect proxy for nutritional status, and higher BMI can coexist with micronutrient deficiencies. The decomposition result is best understood as indicating that nutritional status is strongly socially patterned and statistically linked to the observed inequality in anaemia.

## Socioeconomic inequality

The negative Erreygers index demonstrates that anaemia is concentrated among poorer women rather than being uniformly distributed across Ghanaian society. This is consistent with WHO's broader observation that the burden of anaemia is often greater among rural populations, poorer households, and women with less formal education.

The attenuation of wealth-quintile coefficients after multivariable adjustment should not be mistaken for evidence that socioeconomic circumstances are irrelevant. Wealth is upstream of several characteristics included in the model, including diet, nutritional status, housing conditions, access to care, and geographic residence. Conditioning on these pathways can reduce the apparent independent association of wealth even when the total social gradient remains substantial. The concentration-index framework is therefore especially useful because it captures the distribution of disease across the full socioeconomic ranking rather than relying on a single conditional regression coefficient.

## Geographic and community-level heterogeneity

Regional prevalence varied widely, from roughly 30% in Bono to more than 50% in Oti. Regional differences also persisted in the adjusted model. These findings suggest that national estimates obscure meaningful geographic heterogeneity.

The multilevel analysis supported this conclusion. Approximately 4.4% of the latent variance in anaemia was attributable to differences between clusters in the empty model, falling to about 3.1% after adjustment. Although the ICC appears numerically modest, the corresponding median odds ratio remained about 1.37 after adjustment, indicating non-trivial residual contextual heterogeneity. Measured individual and regional characteristics explained approximately 29% of the between-cluster variance, leaving room for unmeasured contextual factors such as local food environments, malaria transmission, health-service access, sanitation, environmental exposures, or other community-level conditions.

## Public-health implications

These findings argue against a one-size-fits-all anaemia strategy. Ghana's persistently high national burden appears to be shaped by overlapping socioeconomic, nutritional, reproductive, and geographic inequalities.

Interventions should continue to prioritize pregnant women and nutritionally vulnerable women, but the results also suggest a need for geographically targeted strategies in high-burden regions and communities. Because anaemia is multifactorial, programmes focused only on iron supplementation are unlikely to address the full burden. WHO recommends multisectoral approaches that account for nutritional deficiencies, infection and inflammation, reproductive health, and social determinants.

The strong socioeconomic patterning also supports routine monitoring of inequality rather than national prevalence alone. Tracking concentration indices or comparable equity measures alongside overall prevalence could help determine whether progress is benefiting disadvantaged women or merely improving national averages.

## Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It uses recent nationally representative data; incorporates sampling weights, clustering, and stratification; distinguishes population-level inequality from conditional regression associations; applies a concentration-index decomposition; and quantifies residual community-level heterogeneity.

Several limitations should also be considered. First, the cross-sectional design prevents causal inference and does not establish temporal ordering between anaemia and its correlates. Second, haemoglobin identifies anaemia but does not determine its aetiology. The data therefore cannot distinguish iron-deficiency anaemia from anaemia due to malaria, inflammation, haemoglobinopathies, other micronutrient deficiencies, or blood loss. Third, BMI is a crude measure of nutritional status and does not directly measure iron stores or dietary quality. Fourth, some potentially important biological and environmental determinants were unavailable or not measured for the full analytic sample. Fifth, the decomposition is model-dependent and its percentages should be interpreted as statistical contributions rather than causal mediation effects.

Finally, WHO updated its haemoglobin cut-offs and measurement guidance in 2024. The primary analysis uses the classification embedded in the 2022 DHS, which preserves comparability with the survey's official estimates. Future work could evaluate the extent to which newer thresholds alter prevalence and inequality estimates.

## Conclusion

Anaemia remains highly prevalent among women of reproductive age in Ghana and is unequally distributed across socioeconomic and geographic groups. Poorer women bear a disproportionate share of the burden, with nutritional status accounting for a large portion of the measured wealth-related inequality. Pregnancy and regional location remain important correlates, and meaningful community-level heterogeneity persists after adjustment. Reducing anaemia in Ghana will therefore require not only population-wide interventions but also equity-focused, nutritionally informed, and geographically targeted strategies.
