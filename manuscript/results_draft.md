# Results Draft

## Study population and anaemia prevalence

The Ghana 2022 Individual Recode file contained 15,014 women aged 15–49 years. Of these, 7,557 had a valid anaemia classification and comprised the primary analytic sample. After application of the DHS sampling weights, the overall prevalence of anaemia was **41.1% (95% CI 39.6%–42.6%)**.

Anaemia prevalence varied across socioeconomic, reproductive, nutritional, and geographic characteristics. A clear wealth gradient was observed: prevalence was **46.6% (95% CI 43.5%–49.7%)** among women in the poorest wealth quintile and **38.8% (95% CI 35.2%–42.5%)** among women in the richest quintile. Prevalence similarly declined with educational attainment, from **45.4% (95% CI 42.6%–48.3%)** among women with no formal education to **36.8% (95% CI 32.4%–41.2%)** among those with higher education.

Rural women had a higher prevalence of anaemia than urban women (**43.4% vs 39.4%**). Anaemia was also more common among currently pregnant women than among women who were not pregnant or were unsure of their pregnancy status (**51.4% vs 40.4%**). By nutritional status, prevalence was highest among underweight women (**49.3%, 95% CI 44.1%–54.6%**) and lowest among women with obesity (**33.9%, 95% CI 30.5%–37.3%**).

## Socioeconomic inequality

The standard concentration index for anaemia was **-0.0358**, while the Erreygers corrected concentration index was **-0.0589**. A stratified primary-sampling-unit bootstrap yielded a 95% confidence interval of **-0.0914 to -0.0212** for the Erreygers index. The negative value indicates that anaemia was disproportionately concentrated among socioeconomically disadvantaged women.

The concentration curve lay predominantly above the line of equality. For example, the poorest 20% of the weighted population accounted for approximately **22.4%** of the cumulative anaemia burden, while the poorest 40% accounted for approximately **43.5%**.

## Decomposition of wealth-related inequality

Decomposition of the Erreygers concentration index indicated that BMI and nutritional status made the largest measured contribution to wealth-related anaemia inequality, accounting for approximately **77.7%** of the observed index in the reproducible specification. Education contributed approximately **15.6%**, followed by residence (**6.8%**), parity (**4.9%**), and pregnancy status (**3.8%**). Age, employment, and marital/union status made small offsetting contributions, and the residual component offset part of the explained inequality.

In a 200-replicate stratified PSU bootstrap, the BMI/nutritional-status contribution remained consistently negative, with an approximate 95% interval of **-0.0617 to -0.0300**. Bootstrap intervals for the smaller domains included zero, so their individual magnitudes should be interpreted cautiously.

## Adjusted associations with anaemia

In the survey-weighted multivariable logistic model, current pregnancy, BMI category, and region showed the strongest overall evidence of association with anaemia. Currently pregnant women had **77% higher adjusted odds** of anaemia than women who were not pregnant or were unsure of their pregnancy status (aOR **1.77, 95% CI 1.43–2.21**).

Compared with women of normal weight, women who were overweight had lower adjusted odds of anaemia (aOR **0.73, 95% CI 0.62–0.87**) and women with obesity had substantially lower adjusted odds (aOR **0.60, 95% CI 0.49–0.72**). Underweight women had higher estimated odds than normal-weight women, but the confidence interval included the null (aOR **1.18, 95% CI 0.96–1.47**).

Regional differences persisted after adjustment. Relative to Greater Accra, women in Oti had higher adjusted odds of anaemia (aOR **1.48, 95% CI 1.11–1.98**), whereas women in Bono had lower adjusted odds (aOR **0.61, 95% CI 0.41–0.90**).

In contrast, the crude socioeconomic gradients were strongly attenuated in the fully adjusted model. The overall Wald tests for wealth quintile and education were not statistically significant. This pattern is consistent with the decomposition analysis, which suggests that observed socioeconomic inequality may be expressed through the unequal distribution of nutritional, reproductive, and geographic characteristics rather than through an isolated conditional wealth effect.

## Geographic and community-level heterogeneity

Survey-weighted regional anaemia prevalence ranged from approximately **30.1% in Bono** to **51.8% in Oti**, demonstrating substantial geographic variation.

In the empty two-level logistic model, the estimated cluster-level variance was **0.150**, corresponding to an intraclass correlation coefficient of approximately **4.35%** and a median odds ratio of **1.45**. After adjustment for individual, socioeconomic, reproductive, nutritional, and regional characteristics, cluster-level variance decreased to **0.107**, with an adjusted ICC of approximately **3.14%** and MOR of **1.37**.

The proportional change in cluster-level variance was approximately **28.8%**, indicating that the measured covariates explained part, but not all, of the between-community variation in anaemia.

## Overall interpretation

Taken together, the findings indicate that anaemia among women of reproductive age in Ghana is both socioeconomically and geographically patterned. The burden is concentrated among poorer women, but this inequality appears to operate largely through the socioeconomic distribution of nutritional status and other characteristics. Persistent regional and cluster-level heterogeneity after adjustment suggests that contextual factors beyond measured individual characteristics may also contribute to anaemia disparities.
