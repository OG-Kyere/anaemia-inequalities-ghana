# Conclusion Draft

Anaemia remains a substantial public-health problem among women of reproductive age in Ghana, affecting approximately two in five women. The burden is not equally distributed: poorer women experience a disproportionate share of anaemia, and marked differences are evident across regions and communities.

The findings suggest that socioeconomic inequality in anaemia is expressed less through an isolated wealth effect than through the unequal distribution of nutritional, reproductive, educational, and geographic characteristics. Nutritional status, captured through BMI, accounted for the largest measured contribution to wealth-related inequality, while pregnancy and regional location remained important correlates after adjustment.

The persistence of residual community-level heterogeneity also indicates that unmeasured contextual factors may influence anaemia risk. National strategies should therefore combine broad prevention efforts with targeted interventions for socioeconomically disadvantaged women, nutritionally vulnerable groups, pregnant women, and high-burden regions.

Future research should investigate the biological causes of anaemia more directly using iron-status, infection, inflammation, and haemoglobinopathy biomarkers, and should evaluate how newer WHO haemoglobin thresholds affect estimates of prevalence and inequality.
